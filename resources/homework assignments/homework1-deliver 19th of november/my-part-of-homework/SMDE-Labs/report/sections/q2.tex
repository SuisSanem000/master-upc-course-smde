\subsection{Distributions by Age Group and Performance Class}\label{sec:q2a}

\task{Check the distribution of the variables ``body fat (\%), diastolic blood pressure, number of sit ups in a set time, broad jump distance (cm)'' for each age group and for each performance class.
Interpret your findings.}

\paragraph{Data.}
We analyzed the four variables (\texttt{body\_fat\_pct}, \texttt{diastolic}, \texttt{sit\_ups} and \texttt{broad\_jump\_cm}) on the 12,742 observations of the cleaned data set (Section~\ref{sec:q1b}), grouped by \texttt{age\_cat} (Section~\ref{sec:q1e}) and by \texttt{class}.

\paragraph{Distributions by \texttt{age\_cat}.}
The summary statistics and boxplots across the four levels of \texttt{age\_cat} are shown in \autoref{tab:q2a-summary-age} and \autoref{fig:q2a-boxplots-age}.
The mean of \texttt{body\_fat\_pct} increases with age, barely changing between \texttt{<30} and \texttt{30-39} and rising more markedly from age 40 upward (from 21.60\% in \texttt{<30} to 25.93\% in \texttt{50+}).
The mean of \texttt{diastolic} increases slightly from \texttt{<30} (77.47\,mmHg) to \texttt{40-49} (81.82\,mmHg) and remains almost unchanged in \texttt{50+}.
Conversely, \texttt{sit\_ups} decreases steadily across every age group, from a mean of 46.39 in \texttt{<30} down to 27.35 in \texttt{50+}.
The mean of \texttt{broad\_jump\_cm} is nearly the same in \texttt{<30} and \texttt{30-39} (around 204\,cm), and then drops sharply in \texttt{40-49} and \texttt{50+} (down to 160.19\,cm).

\paragraph{Distributions by \texttt{class}.}
The summary statistics and boxplots across the four levels of \texttt{class} (\texttt{D} $<$ \texttt{C} $<$ \texttt{B} $<$ \texttt{A}) are shown in \autoref{tab:q2a-summary-class} and \autoref{fig:q2a-boxplots-class}.
As the performance class improves from \texttt{D} to \texttt{A}, the mean of \texttt{body\_fat\_pct} decreases from 26.88\% in \texttt{D} to 20.51\% in \texttt{A}, with the largest drop between \texttt{D} and \texttt{C}.
The distribution of \texttt{diastolic} is nearly identical across all four classes, with its mean changing by less than 2\,mmHg between \texttt{D} and \texttt{A}.
In contrast, both \texttt{sit\_ups} and \texttt{broad\_jump\_cm} increase steadily from \texttt{D} to \texttt{A} (from 30.67 to 47.91 for \texttt{sit\_ups}, and from 176.56 to 203.01\,cm for \texttt{broad\_jump\_cm}).

\paragraph{Shape and spread.}
In every group of both factors, the mean and the median are very close (\autoref{tab:q2a-summary-age} and \autoref{tab:q2a-summary-class}), indicating approximately symmetric distributions within each group.
The standard deviations are also similar across groups, with the ratio between the largest and smallest group variances remaining below 2 in all cases (\autoref{tab:q2b-assumptions}, column \texttt{Var\_ratio}, at most 1.86 for \texttt{sit\_ups} by \texttt{class}).

\subsection{Effect of Age Group and Performance Class (One-Way ANOVA)}\label{sec:q2b}

\task{Analyze whether the variables ``diastolic blood pressure'', ``body fat'', ``number of sit ups'' and ``broad jump distance'' are affected from age factor (age\_cat)'' and from performance class by using proper method.
Eliminate outlying observations if required and test the assumptions of the statistical method by using corresponding tests and plots.
Write your conclusions.}

\paragraph{Outliers.}
We did not remove any additional outlying observations before fitting the models.
Section~\ref{sec:q1b} already removed the impossible values and those outside the adult reference ranges, and the remaining extreme points in \autoref{fig:q2a-boxplots-age} and \autoref{fig:q2a-boxplots-class} are plausible values.

\paragraph{One-way ANOVA models.}
To test whether the group means of each variable are equal across the four levels of \texttt{age\_cat} and of \texttt{class} ($H_0\colon \mu_1 = \mu_2 = \mu_3 = \mu_4$ against $H_1\colon$ at least one mean differs), we fitted the eight one-way ANOVA models (\autoref{tab:q2b-anova}).
All eight models reject $H_0$ at a 95\% confidence level ($p < 10^{-16} < 0.05$ for seven models, and $p = 2.58 \times 10^{-13} < 0.05$ for \texttt{diastolic} $\sim$ \texttt{class}).
Because with 12,742 observations even very small differences are statistically significant, we also report $\eta^2 = \mathrm{SSB}/\mathrm{SST}$, the share of the total variability explained by the factor (\autoref{tab:q2b-anova}, column \texttt{Eta\_sq}).
By \texttt{age\_cat}, the factor explains 30.0\% of the variability of \texttt{sit\_ups} and 21.0\% of \texttt{broad\_jump\_cm}, but only 6.4\% of \texttt{body\_fat\_pct} and 3.2\% of \texttt{diastolic}.
By \texttt{class}, it explains 19.7\% of the variability of \texttt{sit\_ups}, 11.4\% of \texttt{body\_fat\_pct} and 6.1\% of \texttt{broad\_jump\_cm}, and a negligible 0.5\% of \texttt{diastolic}.

\paragraph{Assumptions.}
We checked the assumptions of the eight models on their residuals (\autoref{tab:q2b-assumptions} and \autoref{fig:q2b-qqplots}):
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Normality of the residuals:} the Shapiro-Wilk test on a random sample of 5,000 residuals rejects normality at a 95\% confidence level in all eight models ($p \leq 2.87 \times 10^{-10} < 0.05$), but the Q-Q plots follow the normal line closely except in the tails (\autoref{fig:q2b-qqplots}), and with large group sizes ANOVA is robust to mild departures from normality.
  \item \textbf{Homoscedasticity:} the Levene test rejects equal variances at a 95\% confidence level in seven models ($p < 0.05$), except for \texttt{diastolic} $\sim$ \texttt{class} ($p = 0.0618 > 0.05$), and the Breusch-Pagan test rejects it in seven models, except for \texttt{broad\_jump\_cm} $\sim$ \texttt{age\_cat} ($p = 0.199 > 0.05$).
    However, the ratio between the largest and smallest group variances is between 1.06 and 1.86 (\autoref{tab:q2b-assumptions}, column \texttt{Var\_ratio}), below the rule-of-thumb threshold of 2 from the course slides, so the heteroscedasticity detected by the tests is small.
  \item \textbf{Independence of the residuals:} the Durbin-Watson test does not reject the absence of autocorrelation at a 95\% confidence level in seven models ($p \geq 0.224 > 0.05$), and barely rejects it in \texttt{sit\_ups} $\sim$ \texttt{age\_cat} ($p = 0.0201 < 0.05$).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Post-hoc tests.}
Since all eight ANOVA models reject $H_0$, we applied three post-hoc tests to compare the $\binom{4}{2} = 6$ pairs of groups in each model: Tukey HSD, Bonferroni and LSD (\autoref{tab:q2b-posthoc-age} for \texttt{age\_cat}, \autoref{tab:q2b-posthoc-class} for \texttt{class}, and the summary count of significant pairs in \autoref{tab:q2b-posthoc-summary}).
Across all models, LSD gives the smallest p-values because it does not adjust for multiple comparisons, Bonferroni gives the largest p-values as the most conservative method, and Tukey HSD lies between the two.

For five of the eight models (\texttt{diastolic} and \texttt{sit\_ups} by \texttt{age\_cat}, and \texttt{body\_fat\_pct}, \texttt{sit\_ups} and \texttt{broad\_jump\_cm} by \texttt{class}), all three post-hoc tests agree that all 6 pairs of groups differ significantly at a 95\% confidence level (\autoref{tab:q2b-posthoc-summary}).
For \texttt{broad\_jump\_cm} $\sim$ \texttt{age\_cat}, all three tests agree that 5 of the 6 pairs differ significantly, while \texttt{30-39} vs \texttt{<30} (a difference of 1.23\,cm) is not significant in any test ($p \geq 0.139 > 0.05$).
The three post-hoc methods disagree at the $\alpha = 0.05$ threshold in only two small-difference pairs:
\begin{itemize}
  \item In \texttt{body\_fat\_pct} $\sim$ \texttt{age\_cat}, the pair \texttt{30-39} vs \texttt{<30} (difference 0.36\%) is significant according to LSD ($p = 0.0246 < 0.05$), but not according to Tukey HSD ($p = 0.111 > 0.05$) or Bonferroni ($p = 0.148 > 0.05$).
  \item In \texttt{diastolic} $\sim$ \texttt{class}, all three tests find no difference between \texttt{B} and \texttt{C} (0.08\,mmHg, $p \geq 0.745 > 0.05$), while the pair \texttt{A} vs \texttt{C} ($-0.59$\,mmHg) is significant according to LSD ($p = 0.0157 < 0.05$) but not according to Tukey HSD ($p = 0.0741 > 0.05$) or Bonferroni ($p = 0.0941 > 0.05$).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Conclusions.}
Both \texttt{age\_cat} and \texttt{class} have a statistically significant effect on all four variables, with the largest effects on \texttt{sit\_ups} and \texttt{broad\_jump\_cm}, a moderate effect on \texttt{body\_fat\_pct}, and a very small effect on \texttt{diastolic}.
Controlling for multiple comparisons with Tukey HSD and Bonferroni avoids the false positives of unadjusted LSD on the small differences between \texttt{<30} and \texttt{30-39} for \texttt{body\_fat\_pct} and between classes \texttt{A} and \texttt{C} for \texttt{diastolic}.

\subsection{Effect of Age and Gender Together (Two-Way ANOVA)}\label{sec:q2c}

\task{Analyze the effect of age and gender together on number of sit ups and broad jump distance.
Analyze whether the interaction of two term is significant.
Interpret your findings.}

\paragraph{Two-way ANOVA models.}
We fitted the two-way ANOVA models with interaction, \texttt{sit\_ups} $\sim$ \texttt{age\_cat} $*$ \texttt{gender} and \texttt{broad\_jump\_cm} $\sim$ \texttt{age\_cat} $*$ \texttt{gender}.
Because the eight \texttt{age\_cat} $\times$ \texttt{gender} groups are unbalanced and the two factors are not independent (Section~\ref{sec:q1f}), we tested the main effects with Type~II F-tests, where each main effect is adjusted for the other (\autoref{tab:q2c-anova}).

For both \texttt{sit\_ups} and \texttt{broad\_jump\_cm}, the interaction \texttt{age\_cat:gender} is statistically significant at a 95\% confidence level ($p = 4.17 \times 10^{-15} < 0.05$ for \texttt{sit\_ups} and $p = 1.99 \times 10^{-6} < 0.05$ for \texttt{broad\_jump\_cm}), alongside the main effects of \texttt{age\_cat} and \texttt{gender} ($p < 10^{-16} < 0.05$ in both models).
However, the partial $\eta^2$ of the interaction is very small (0.5\% for \texttt{sit\_ups} and 0.2\% for \texttt{broad\_jump\_cm}), whereas the main effects of \texttt{age\_cat} (partial $\eta^2$ of 33.9\% and 31.7\%) and \texttt{gender} (26.7\% and 57.5\%) account for the vast majority of the explained variability.

\paragraph{Assumptions.}
We checked the assumptions of the two models on their residuals across the eight \texttt{age\_cat} $\times$ \texttt{gender} cells (\autoref{tab:q2c-assumptions} and \autoref{fig:q2c-qqplots}):
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Normality of the residuals:} the Shapiro-Wilk test rejects normality at a 95\% confidence level in both models ($p \leq 1.96 \times 10^{-16} < 0.05$), but the Q-Q plots follow the normal line closely except in the tails (\autoref{fig:q2c-qqplots}).
  \item \textbf{Homoscedasticity:} the Levene and Breusch-Pagan tests reject equal variances at a 95\% confidence level in both models ($p \leq 2.92 \times 10^{-12} < 0.05$), but the ratio between the largest and smallest cell variances is only 1.43 for \texttt{sit\_ups} and 1.41 for \texttt{broad\_jump\_cm} (\autoref{tab:q2c-assumptions}, column \texttt{Var\_ratio}), well below 2.
  \item \textbf{Independence of the residuals:} the Durbin-Watson test does not reject the absence of autocorrelation for \texttt{broad\_jump\_cm} ($p = 0.854 > 0.05$) and barely rejects it for \texttt{sit\_ups} ($p = 0.0461 < 0.05$).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Interpretation and post-hoc comparisons.}
Because the interaction is significant, we examined the effect of \texttt{age\_cat} separately within each level of \texttt{gender} using the interaction plots (\autoref{fig:q2c-interaction}) and one-way post-hoc comparisons per gender (\autoref{tab:q2c-posthoc}).
In every age group, men have higher means than women in both \texttt{sit\_ups} and \texttt{broad\_jump\_cm}, and both variables decrease with age in each gender (\autoref{fig:q2c-interaction}).
Within each gender, all 6 pairs of age groups differ significantly at a 95\% confidence level according to Tukey HSD, Bonferroni and LSD for both \texttt{sit\_ups} and \texttt{broad\_jump\_cm} (\autoref{tab:q2c-posthoc}).

The interaction appears primarily between \texttt{<30} and \texttt{30-39}, where the lines in \autoref{fig:q2c-interaction} are less parallel because the decline from \texttt{<30} to \texttt{30-39} is steeper for women than for men.
Specifically, from \texttt{<30} to \texttt{30-39}, the mean of \texttt{sit\_ups} drops by $-6.15$ in women against $-2.62$ in men, and the mean of \texttt{broad\_jump\_cm} drops by $-8.55$\,cm in women against only $-2.59$\,cm in men (\autoref{tab:q2c-posthoc}).
After age 40, the lines of both genders decrease with similar slopes down to \texttt{50+}.
